X-Wheel:
Safe Control of a
Balancing Wheel

A commercial self-balancing one-wheel robot is a closed system: it keeps itself upright, and you can't change its controller. We made it steerable by adding a lead-screw weight shift for forward and backward motion and a reaction wheel for turning, then built a safety supervisor around how those actuators actually behave. Second author on the paper (arXiv:2610.16223).

Context

Commercial balancing wheels stay upright through an internal controller and are normally steered by a rider leaning. Treating that controller as a black box, the question was how to drive the wheel autonomously and safely using only actuators added on top.

Forward and backward motion comes from a lead screw that shifts weight, which the robot's own balance controller turns into motion. Turning comes from a reaction wheel. The reaction wheel holds limited momentum, so turns happen as a series of short torque pulses, and whether a pulse actually rotates the body depends on static friction at the tire.

That makes turning uncertain by nature. The work models that uncertainty from real torque sweeps and builds a supervisor that stays safe in spite of it.

System Architecture

The system runs from embedded actuation up to a supervisor that decides when to pulse, when to brake, and how wide its safety margins should be.

Stage 1 — Actuation

ESP32-S3 Firmware over CAN

C++ firmware on an ESP32-S3 commands an ODrive FOC motor controller over CAN for the reaction wheel and weight-shift actuators. Blocking serial I/O was causing control-loop jitter, and removing it made actuation timing consistent.

Stage 2 — Localization

Overhead-Camera AprilTag Tracking

An overhead Orbbec Femto Bolt tracks AprilTags on the robot to give position and heading during experiments. Getting it right meant fixing a mirrored heading frame and handling a 180° pose ambiguity.

Stage 3 — Modeling

System ID and Friction-Gated Turning

Actuator dynamics were identified from step responses with least squares. For turning, a two-part model estimates the probability that a pulse rotates the body and the rotation it produces. The weight shift is modeled as a rate-limited input, which predicts much longer stopping distances than treating it as a direct force.

Stage 4 — Control

Nonlinear MPC and Event-Triggered Supervisor

A nonlinear MPC respects actuator slew and travel limits. The supervisor schedules turning pulses, brakes when the predicted stopping path would leave the safe region, widens margins to cover model uncertainty, and updates its friction estimate online.

Hardware

Balancing Wheel

A commercial self-balancing one-wheel robot whose internal balance controller is treated as a black box.

Lead-Screw Weight Shift

Moves mass forward or back so the wheel's own balance controller drives it longitudinally.

Reaction Wheel

Produces yaw torque in short pulses. Limited stored momentum is why turning is pulse-based.

ESP32-S3 + ODrive

Embedded controller running C++ firmware, talking to an ODrive FOC motor driver over CAN.

Orbbec Femto Bolt

Overhead depth camera used with AprilTags to track the robot's position and heading during experiments.

Torque Sweeps

Repeated pulses across torque levels on one surface, used to fit the friction-gated turning model.
